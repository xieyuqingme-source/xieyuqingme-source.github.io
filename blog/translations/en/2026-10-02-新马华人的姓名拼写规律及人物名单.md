# Spelling Patterns in Singaporean and Malaysian Chinese Names, with Lists of Notable Figures

One limitation of phonetic writing systems is that the same character can acquire different pronunciations after communities have been separated for only a few decades or a century. Combine this with different spelling rules, and the written forms eventually differ too.

The spelling of overseas Chinese surnames offers a glimpse of this. Take my own surname, “谢”: in Hanyu Pinyin it is “Xie”; in Taiwan's Wade–Giles system, “Hsieh”; in Hong Kong, following Cantonese pronunciation and English spelling conventions, “Tse”; in Macau, following Cantonese pronunciation and Portuguese spelling conventions, “Che”; in Vietnamese, using the Sino-Vietnamese reading, “Tạ”; and in Korean, using the Sino-Korean reading, "Sa". Singapore and Malaysia are more complicated still: the possibilities are “Cheah/Tay/Chia” and “Cheah/Chiah/Chia/Seah/Sia/See/Tia,” respectively.

Singapore and Malaysia have so many spellings because many dialects are spoken there, including Cantonese, Hakka, and Teochew/Hokkien. Literary and colloquial readings add further variation, so one character can have several spellings, and one spelling does not necessarily identify a single surname. “Tan,” for example, might mean “谭,” but is more likely to mean “陈,” following Teochew/Hokkien pronunciation.

The basic rule for Chinese names in Singapore and Malaysia is therefore to spell the sounds of one's own dialect using English. Some younger ethnic Chinese now use Hanyu Pinyin for their given names, but still follow their fathers' surname spellings. Otherwise, Malay clerks registering names would find it strange for one family to have different surnames. Fortunately, Chinese traditions are well preserved in both Singapore and Malaysia. People have their own names in Chinese characters; the romanized form is only an aid to help people of other ethnicities address them.

Unfortunately, I do not speak these dialects, so it is hard for me to analyze the pronunciations behind the spellings. But I have another method: listing examples. Once we have seen enough names, some patterns should naturally become clear.

For this investigation, I compiled the following two sets of reference data. Table One is an AI-assisted list of representative Chinese figures in Singapore and Malaysia, compiled from publicly available historical material. Table Two contains surname-spelling samples for 206 Chinese pioneers, obtained by cleaning and filtering the Singapore Biographical Database (SBDB), removing people without distinctive English names. Not everyone in this table is Singaporean; many merely have connections with Singapore.

After reading them, my impressions are: ① I cannot find a pattern; ② Mandarin and Hanyu Pinyin are still easier to understand; ③ perhaps someone proficient in Cantonese, Hokkien, Hakka, and English could spot something.
