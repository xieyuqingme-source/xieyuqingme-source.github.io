# Questions and Answers on Capital 1.6: How Should We Understand a Commodity's Exchange-Value and Value, and Their Relationship?

## Question:

How should we understand a commodity's exchange-value? How should we understand its value? What is the relationship between the two?

## Answer:

A commodity's exchange-value and value, and their relationship, can be understood from the following perspectives.

1 **The definition of exchange-value.** Marx says: “Exchange-value first appears as a quantitative relation, the proportion in which use-values of one kind exchange for use-values of another kind, a relation constantly changing with time and place. (49)” For example, if I can exchange five jin of rice for two of your garments, we can say that two garments are the exchange-value of five jin of rice.

2 **The definition of value.** For rice and garments to be exchangeable, they must have something in common: the undifferentiated human labor congealed in the commodities, which is value. Apart from their natural properties, their only common feature is that both are products of labor.

> “The common element that manifests itself in the exchange relation, or exchange-value, of commodities is their value. (51)”

3 **The relationship between exchange-value and value.** Value is the basis of exchange-value. Value is a commodity's inherent property, while exchange-value is merely its form of expression.

4 **The dual character of commodities.** A commodity can therefore be said to possess two properties—the two factors of a commodity—use-value and value. It is a unity of use-value and value.

> “Commodities appear in the form of use-values, or commodity-bodies, such as iron, linen, wheat, and so on. This is their ordinary, natural form. However, they are commodities only because they are something twofold: both useful objects and bearers of value. They therefore appear as commodities, or possess the commodity form, only insofar as they possess a double form, a natural form and a value-form. (61)”

5 Value is a social property peculiar to commodities, so products and services that do not enter the market have no value. For instance, **services performed through household labor** are not commodities and have no value because they do not enter the market or serve exchange; they possess only use-value. **Paid domestic services**, by contrast, are commodities possessing both value and use-value. Of course, “value” here is simply a term in Marxist political economy, carrying no moral implications.

6 A commodity's value is a social property and can therefore manifest itself only in social relations between commodities. Thus, we **cannot study individual commodities and individual people metaphysically, in isolation**.

> “No producer, whether in industry or agriculture, produces value or commodities when considered in isolation. His product becomes value and a commodity only within particular social connections. (Volume III, 719)”
